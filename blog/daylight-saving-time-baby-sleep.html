Daylight Saving Time and Baby Sleep: A Gentle Fall Back Plan

By Jordan · October 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Here is the funny thing about the end of daylight saving time: everyone tells you it is the good one. You get an extra hour! Then Sunday morning arrives, and your sweet baby is bouncing in the crib at what the clock insists is 5:15 a.m. If that sounds familiar, or if you are bracing for it this year, mama, take a breath. This one is very manageable.

In most of the U.S., clocks fall back one hour at 2 a.m. on Sunday, November 1. This guide walks through why it throws little ones off, three ways to handle it, and what to do if the early mornings stick around for a bit.

Why Your Baby Does Not Care What the Clock Says

Babies and toddlers run on an internal body clock, and that clock is tuned to light, feeding times, and routine, not to the number on your microwave. So when the clocks move, your child's body keeps right on going. A baby who normally wakes at 6:00 a.m. will still wake when it feels like 6:00, which is suddenly 5:00 on the clock.

The same shift hits naps and bedtime. A 7:00 p.m. bedtime now feels like 8:00 p.m. to their body, so you may see a little overtired energy in the evening. Most families see things settle within a week or so, though it can be a little bumpy while it does.

Good news: falling back is generally considered the easier direction. Shifting later is gentler on little bodies than shifting earlier, which is why spring is usually the rougher one.

Pick Your Approach

There is no single right way here. The veteran moms I text are split almost evenly between "just change the clocks and go" and "ease in over a few days," and both camps have happy babies. Choose what fits your child and your week.

A Four Day Gradual Plan

If you like having a plan on paper (I do), here is a simple one. It moves wake time, naps, meals, and bedtime together, so the whole day shifts as a unit.

  1. Thursday, October 29: Move everything about 15 minutes later than usual.
  2. Friday, October 30: Move everything about 30 minutes later than your normal times.
  3. Saturday, October 31: Move everything about 45 minutes later. Halloween may make this happen on its own, with costumes and a later dinner!
  4. Sunday, November 1: Follow the new clock. Your usual 7:00 a.m. wake up is now just 7:00 a.m. again.

You do not have to be exact. Close counts. If a nap runs ten minutes off or dinner is late, nothing is ruined. The goal is a gentle slope instead of a cliff.

If you would rather switch all at once

On Sunday morning, treat the new clock as the truth. If your baby wakes at what feels like 5:30 a.m. to them, try to wait it out a little before starting the day, keeping the room dark and quiet. Offer naps and bedtime by the new clock, even if it feels a little early for them at first. Then give it several days to catch up.

Protecting the Morning

The biggest complaint after a time change is the early wake up. A few things help more than anything else I have heard from other moms or read about.

Make the room truly dark

Light is the strongest signal your child's body gets about when the day starts. Sunrise will arrive an hour earlier on the clock after we fall back, so a dark room matters even more. I highly recommend blackout curtains, and a small piece of tape or a clip to seal gaps around the edges. Tuck the nightlight away if it is bright, or choose a dim, warm one.

Add a little white noise

Early morning is the lightest part of sleep, and household sounds like a dog or a coffee maker can pull babies awake. A steady sound machine, set at a safe, moderate volume and placed well away from the crib, can help cover the little noises.

Wait a few minutes before rushing in

If your baby wakes early but sounds fine, give a couple of minutes before you go in. Some babies chat, fuss briefly, and drift back off. Other babies are clearly hungry or upset, and that is your call to make. You know your child best.

For toddlers, try a gentle wake up cue

Older toddlers can sometimes learn that they wait in their bed until a light or clock changes color. A simple toddler clock (many of them are inexpensive) turns "is it morning yet?" into something concrete. Celebrate every success, even a small one. Our guide to toddler sleep schedules has more ideas for building a routine around wake times.

Handling Naps and Bedtime

It is tempting to fix a rough morning by putting your child to bed much earlier. Try to resist that. An earlier bedtime can backfire for some babies and lead to an even earlier wake up, because there is only so much sleep they need overnight. Instead, move bedtime in small steps, and keep the last nap of the day from running long.

If bedtime was a little late during the transition and your child melts down, stay calm and keep the routine short and cozy. Bath, book, song, and into the crib. The familiar order of things is comforting, and it signals sleep no matter what the clock reads. Consistency is the thing that carries you through. If you are working on routines more generally, our gentle sleep training guide goes deeper on building them.

Use light on your side

Get outside in the morning when you can. Natural daylight helps reset the body clock. In the evening, dim the lights and turn off screens an hour or so before bed. These little habits help the new schedule sink in faster.

What About Newborns?

Very young babies have not developed a steady day and night rhythm yet, so there is not much to adjust. For newborns, skip the elaborate plan. Feed when they are hungry, sleep when they are sleepy, and let the new clock settle into your days. It will sort itself out. Whenever you are putting a baby down, keep following safe sleep guidance from the American Academy of Pediatrics: a firm, flat surface, on the back, with nothing else in the crib.

Is It a Time Change or Something Else?

Around the same stage, many babies also hit a developmental leap or sleep regression, and the timing can make it confusing. If your baby is between about four months and two years old and sleep falls apart for longer than you would expect, it may be more than the clock. Our post on baby sleep regressions can help you tell the difference.

Most time change troubles ease up in about a week to two weeks. If your child is still struggling after that, or if you are worried about snoring, breathing pauses, or very restless sleep, check in with your pediatrician. The CDC's sleep resources are also a helpful place to start for general sleep health.

Do Not Forget About You

Sleep disruption is hard on parents too, and the extra hour of sleep that clocks promise can feel pretty unreal when a baby is up at 5. I am someone who struggles to accept help, so I will say this the way I wish someone had said it to me: let people help you. If your partner is around, trade mornings so each of you gets a sleep in. If you have a village, lean on it for an hour of quiet. Rest is not a luxury, it is part of how you show up for your little one.

A rough week of mornings is just a week. Your baby is not broken, and neither is your routine.

You have got this, mama. A little prep, a dark room, and some patience will carry you through the time change, and soon enough everyone will be back to a rhythm that works. If you need extra encouragement, send a text to a mom friend who has been there. I promise she has a story!
